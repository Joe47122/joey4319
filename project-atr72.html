01 · Problem & Scope

Why Actuator-Disk Slipstream CFD Matters

Turboprop aircraft experience strongly asymmetric propeller slipstream wash during critical flight phases such as one-engine-inoperative (OEI) climb. Characterising these aerodynamic loads across the wing-nacelle junction is essential for structural sizing, yet fully resolved sliding-mesh rotor simulations require hundreds to thousands of CPU hours.

This thesis modelled the complete ATR 72-600 airframe (wing, fuselage, nacelles, and empennage) in CATIA V5 and simulated it in ANSYS Fluent using the steady Actuator Disk Method (ADMv2) with blade element momentum (BEM) source terms. The scope focused on high-fidelity aerodynamic load extraction and rigorous three-level Grid Convergence Index (GCI) verification; no separate structural FEA solve was performed.

Case A · 116 KCAS, 10° AoA

Symmetric Take-Off Baseline

With both propellers operating at take-off thrust, the slipstream produces a mirrored suction and pressure-recovery envelope across each wing-nacelle junction, with a leading-edge stagnation peak near 3,300 Pa and accelerated local slipstream velocity (~99 m/s vs. 59.1 m/s freestream).

Case B · 115 KCAS, 6° AoA

One-Engine-Inoperative (OEI) Climb

With the failed engine feathered, severe aerodynamic asymmetry develops across the span: the operative wing-nacelle junction reaches a suction peak of −6,752 Pa and turbulent kinetic energy of 35.9 m²/s², while the opposite wing remains in clean, unaccelerated freestream flow.

03 · Computational Benchmark

Why ~3 CPU Hours Matters

Converging the 616k-cell full-aircraft model in roughly 3 CPU hours demonstrates that BEM-informed actuator-disk modelling captures asymmetric OEI junction loads on standard university compute hardware, compared against 170 CPU hours for 42M-cell steady ADM studies (Yang et al., 2024) and thousands of CPU hours for unsteady sliding-mesh simulations (Zhao et al., 2022).
